**Conformità urbanistica** (planning compliance) tells you whether the home as it is today matches what the town council authorised; **conformità catastale** (land registry compliance) tells you whether it matches the floor plan and data held by the land registry. They are two different checks, and before buying a home in Italy it's worth doing both, because a property can be in order with the land registry but not with the council, and vice versa.

This guide is for anyone about to make an offer. I explain the difference between the two, which documents to ask for and from whom, when to have them checked and how an irregularity today can complicate a renovation tomorrow.

*This guide is for information only and is up to date as of October 2026: every property needs checking by a licensed professional, and the rules change.*

## Planning and land registry compliance: the difference

| | Conformità urbanistica | Conformità catastale |
|---|---|---|
| What it compares | The actual state with the building permits filed with the council (licences, concessions, permits, CILA, SCIA) | The actual state with the land registry floor plan and data (category, rooms, rateable value) |
| What it's for | Establishing whether the home is lawful | Calculating taxes and identifying the property |
| At completion | The seller declares the details of the building permits (art. 46 DPR 380/2001) | The seller declares that the floor plan and data match the actual state (art. 29(1-bis), Law 52/1985) |
| If it doesn't comply | A retrospective permit (sanatoria) is needed, if possible; otherwise the irregularity passes to the buyer | A land registry update (DOCFA filing) is needed before completion |

The key point: **the land registry is not proof that the home is lawful**. An up-to-date registry plan can show a wall the council never authorised.

## Stato legittimo and agibilità

The **stato legittimo** (lawful state) is the home as described by its building permits: the one that authorised the construction and those for later works (art. 9-bis(1-bis), DPR 380/2001). The planning check compares this lawful state with the actual state.

For buildings constructed **before 1 September 1967** outside built-up areas, when a permit was often not required, the lawful state is reconstructed from other documents, such as the original land registry plans or archive photos and records.

**Agibilità** (fitness for use, today a certified declaration under art. 24 DPR 380/2001) certifies safety, hygiene, energy efficiency and services. It matters, but **it doesn't replace compliance**: a home with agibilità can have later alterations that were never authorised.

There are also **tolerances**: small measurement differences from the approved design that don't count as irregularities (art. 34-bis DPR 380/2001). The 2024 Salva Casa decree widened them for works carried out by 24 May 2024, to between 2% and 6% depending on the size of the home. A moved wall, however, is not a tolerance.

## What to check before buying: the checklist

| Document | Where it is | Who can request it |
|---|---|---|
| Visura catastale (land registry extract) | Agenzia delle Entrate, online | Anyone |
| Planimetria catastale (land registry floor plan) | Agenzia delle Entrate | The owner or a professional they authorise |
| Building permits (licences, concessions, permits, CILA, SCIA, amnesties) | The council's building archive, through an accesso agli atti (records request) | The owner or an authorised professional |
| Agibilità | The council, with the building permits | The owner or an authorised professional |
| Title deed (previous sale deed, inheritance) | The seller | The seller |
| Certificates for electrical, plumbing and heating systems | The seller | The seller |
| APE (energy performance certificate) | The seller, mandatory for a sale | The seller |
| Building rules, service charges and decisions on works | The building manager | The seller |

The **accesso agli atti** at the council is the slowest step: by law the council has 30 days to respond (Law 241/1990), but real timescales vary a great deal from one council to another.

## Checking before completion: when to do it

1. **Before the offer**: ask for the visura, the floor plan and the APE. If something already looks wrong, it's better to know straight away.
2. **In the offer**: if you like the home, you can add a **condition precedent** linked to the outcome of the technical check, agreed with the seller or agency.
3. **Before the preliminary contract**: the right moment for the full check, including building permits. Once the preliminary is signed and the deposit paid, pulling out is hard.
4. **Before completion**: check that any agreed regularisation has been carried out.

Before the notary, planning compliance is **declared, not verified**: the notary checks ownership, mortgages and the seller's declarations, but doesn't go into the home to compare it with the plans. If you take out a mortgage, the bank's valuation can also hold up the loan if irregularities are found.

## The most common irregularities

In apartments, the irregularities found most often are:

- **internal walls moved, added or removed** without a permit;
- **a bathroom or kitchen moved** to another room;
- **balconies or loggias enclosed** with glazing;
- **lofts, attics or cellars used as habitable rooms**;
- **windows created or altered** on the façade.

They are not all equally serious. Some can be regularised with a sanatoria and a land registry update; others, if the work wasn't allowed, have to be undone. Costs and timescales depend on the case and the council: that's why the assessment needs to happen before signing, not after.

## Compliance and future renovation

This is what I see most in my work, because many clients renovate after buying. To file a building permit you have to start from a lawful state: if the home has an irregularity, **it has to be regularised before renovating**. That means more time, more cost and sometimes a design that needs rethinking. An irregularity found before completion, on the other hand, can be put right by the seller or used to renegotiate the price.

## Who carries out the check

A **licensed professional**: an architect, engineer or geometra registered with their professional body. A good check includes a site visit with measurements and photos, the records request at the council, a comparison between the actual state, building permits and land registry, and a written report saying what is in order, what isn't and how it can be fixed.

The cost depends on how many permits need finding, the size of the home and the council. Be wary of very cheap fixed-price checks that don't include the records request: without the building permits, planning compliance can't be verified.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who pays for the check, the buyer or the seller?

Whoever asks for it. Usually the buyer, for protection; but a seller who has the home checked before putting it on the market reaches completion without surprises and sells faster.

### Doesn't the notary check everything?

No. The notary checks ownership, mortgages and the seller's declarations, but doesn't check in the home that its actual state matches the building permits.

### Can I buy a home with irregularities?

According to Italy's Supreme Court sitting in full (Cassazione a Sezioni Unite, judgment 8230 of 2019), the deed is valid if it cites a real building permit that relates to that home, even if the property then has irregularities. But the irregularity passes to the buyer, along with the cost of regularising it and the problems it causes for renovation, mortgages or a future sale.
